# Which record-brief prototype should a Kerala clinic pilot?

## Decision update after doctor consultation — 13 October 2026

The user reports that the doctors consulted preferred **Prototype 2, timeline first**, because chronological records were easier for them to follow. The timeline is now the selected pilot layout. The number of doctors, their devices, the cases they tried, and task times or error counts were not supplied, so this is a local preference report rather than evidence of superiority across Kerala clinics. The [interactive pilot](prototype-record-brief/README.md) now opens on `?variant=timeline`; its medication review, source checks, access gates, partial processing, correction, and next-visit scenarios remain part of the evaluation.

The research recommendation below was written **before** this direct feedback. It remains as the rationale for the earlier brief-first hypothesis, which the clinic preference has superseded. The next test should measure whether timeline first also helps doctors find the right source and avoid wrong or missed facts on the actual clinic devices.

Research checked: 13 October 2026. Original decision context: the proposed **private General Medicine walk-in clinic**, whose devices, intake capacity, and record software had **not yet been observed** when this research was written. The project assumed that patients usually bring English paper records, with occasional digital files. This report compares the three [fictional-data prototypes](prototype-record-brief/README.md); it does not assess their clinical accuracy or claim to represent every Kerala clinic.

## Earlier research recommendation (before doctor feedback)

**The pre-consultation research hypothesis favored `?variant=brief` (brief-first) for the first demonstration.** Put the current visit reason, a short relevant history, medicines/allergies requiring reconciliation, key recent results, uncertain facts, and capture completeness above the fold. A clinician should be able to open the original page from each consequential fact. For a later iteration, on a sufficiently wide screen allow the source page to stay beside the brief while reviewing it; on a portrait tablet, use an obvious Brief/Source switch that preserves the selected fact. Keep the full timeline one action away for chronology questions. This is a **design inference** from the clinic's stated time-saving goal, local paper-record research, and controlled usability work on problem-oriented retrieval and source inspection—not a finding that brief-first beat the other two prototypes in Kerala. [Kerala patient-held-record study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/hex.13721), [problem-oriented retrieval experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33566093/), [AHRQ primary-care EHR usability framework](https://digital.ahrq.gov/file/26269/download?token=KqxW_WMc), [2026 provenance usability study](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2026/1/e95644)

**Do not freeze the layout before observing the clinic.** If the doctor routinely verifies many photographed prescriptions or lab results while speaking with the patient, split-screen may prove the better default on the clinic's desktop or landscape tablet. If the chief task is tracing medication changes over years, timeline-first may win for that task. The existing split prototype changes to Brief/Original-document tabs below 960 CSS pixels; that is a different verification interaction and needs a separate portrait-tablet test. [Prototype layout description](prototype-record-brief/README.md), [prototype responsive CSS](prototype-record-brief/styles.css)

**What would overturn brief-first:** choose split-screen if, on the clinic's actual device, it reduces source-check time and clinically important misses without increasing total doctor review/documentation time; choose timeline-first if chronology tasks dominate and it improves those measures without slowing the opening assessment. Capture work happens before any doctor layout is shown, so patient/nurse minutes should be compared against the existing paper workflow, not attributed to a particular doctor-view variant. These are proposed decision rules, subject to thresholds agreed with the doctor before testing. [Pilot decision plan](pre-visit-record-brief.md), [clinic observation guide](clinic-discovery-and-pilot-observation.md)

## What the UI research supports

| Direct evidence | Reading for these prototypes | Strength and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| In a randomized-block **EHR simulation**, a problem-oriented view let users retrieve relevant labs and medicines in a mean 173 seconds versus 205 seconds with a standard view; errors were 3.4% versus 7.7%. [Original experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33566093/) | A task-focused opening brief is promising for quick retrieval, provided its clinical content is correct. | This tested a specific structured EHR view and simple retrieval tasks outside Kerala. It did not test AI summaries, photographed paper, or our brief. |
| AHRQ's expert framework recommends a one-screen overview with details quickly available, proximity of related information, low clutter, and apparent source/confidence of displayed data. [AHRQ framework, pp. 15, 24](https://digital.ahrq.gov/file/26269/download?token=KqxW_WMc) | Brief-first plus direct source opening follows these principles; a permanently open document pane helps only if it does not crowd the overview. | This is US expert guidance from 2009, not a comparative trial or a Kerala workflow study. |
| A small ambulatory physician pilot comparing a medication **timeline** with a table reported better accuracy on five history tasks and faster completion on four of five, without statistically significant speed differences. [Original medication-timeline study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7647176/) | Keep timeline exploration strong for past prescriptions, dose changes, and temporal relationships; do not bury chronology in prose. | It studied medication history, not a mixed-document pre-visit brief. The authors describe small, remote, unsupervised testing and weak statistical power. |
| A 2026 formative usability study used a click-to-inspect AI summary: selecting a sentence opened and highlighted its originating note **side by side**. Forty-six clinician interactions with synthetic charts yielded a mean System Usability Scale score of 86.25. [Original study](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2026/1/e95644) | Make source inspection immediate at the individual claim, and offer a persistent source pane during verification. | It measured perceived usability, **not** verification speed, detection of unsupported claims, clinical outcomes, or a head-to-head win for split-screen. The authors disclosed commercial interests in the evaluated interface. |
| In an original 2026 assessment of an EHR-integrated AI chart review tool, 10 physicians supplied free-text feedback on 147 summaries; coded concerns included missing information and confusing content. [Original study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44401-025-00064-x) | A beautiful summary can still omit important facts. Keep capture completeness, corrections, and original pages prominent. | This is qualitative feedback on a different tool and EHR; the counts are feedback codes, not error rates for this product. |
| In a 2025 **preprint** on heart-failure admission summaries, access to an LLM summary did **not** significantly reduce overall question-completion time, though frequent use correlated with faster completion. [Original preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.02.25328807v1) | Do not assume a summary automatically delivers the primary goal of doctor time saved. Measure it directly. | Preprint, inpatient heart-failure context, and neither a Kerala nor a three-layout comparison. |

The evidence supports **both** snapshot and chronology functions, and makes source verification a central interaction. None of these studies directly compares the three supplied prototypes with Kerala General Medicine doctors. The local choice is therefore conditional on task mix, screen, and upload quality. [Problem-oriented retrieval experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33566093/), [medication-timeline study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7647176/), [provenance usability study](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2026/1/e95644)

## Three-way decision

| Prototype | Best fit | Main risk in this clinic | Pilot role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Brief-first** | Fast first pass when the doctor has not met the walk-in patient and must identify today's relevant history and uncertainties. [Problem-oriented retrieval experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33566093/), [AHRQ framework](https://digital.ahrq.gov/file/26269/download?token=KqxW_WMc) | Salient-looking text can hide omissions, uncertain handwriting, or a pile of uncaptured pages. A drawer can add navigation cost when many claims need checking. [AI chart-review feedback study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44401-025-00064-x), [Kerala patient-held-record study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/hex.13721) | Earlier research default; superseded by doctor feedback. |
| **2. Timeline-first** | Reconstructing dated care, medication starts/stops, changing doses, and test trends. [Medication-timeline study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7647176/) | May require more scanning before the doctor reaches the current visit's priorities; mixed paper dates may be missing or wrong. These are **design risks to test**, not measured failures of this prototype. | **Selected clinic pilot** after doctor preference for chronology; test source-finding, omissions, and time on real devices. |
| **3. Split-screen brief + originals** | Repeated source checks of handwritten prescriptions, lab numbers, or discharge details while preserving the brief's context. [Provenance usability study](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2026/1/e95644), [AHRQ framework](https://digital.ahrq.gov/file/26269/download?token=KqxW_WMc) | Pane width may reduce readability, especially on tablet. At portrait widths the existing prototype uses tabs, so side-by-side verification disappears. These are **layout hypotheses** to measure locally. | Source-review mode on desktop/landscape tablet; elevate to default if actual clinicians spend much of the encounter verifying claims. |

## Safety and interaction requirements to carry into the test

1. A brief fact must identify its **source document, date, page, and certainty**; opening it should land at the supporting passage or page. If image text cannot be localized reliably, show the page and label the match unverified rather than presenting a false highlight. Source visibility and confidence cues follow [AHRQ's usability framework](https://digital.ahrq.gov/file/26269/download?token=KqxW_WMc); immediate claim-level inspection is supported as a usable pattern by the [2026 formative study](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2026/1/e95644).
2. Distinguish **“prescribed on this old page,” “patient reports taking,” and “doctor confirmed taking.”** Do not turn an uncertain handwritten drug or dose into a confident current medication. This is a proposed product safety rule consistent with the Kerala study's focus on medication information transfer and with the need to expose source uncertainty. [Kerala patient-held-record study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/hex.13721), [AHRQ framework](https://digital.ahrq.gov/file/26269/download?token=KqxW_WMc)
3. Show processing status and gaps: **pages supplied / captured / processed, blurry pages, unclear dates, late uploads, and possibly absent records**. Do not silently reorder uncertain-date documents or imply that no documented allergy means no allergy. This is a proposed response to incomplete patient-held information reported in the [two-state outpatient study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0207511) and to summary omissions reported in the [AI chart-review feedback study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44401-025-00064-x).
4. Preserve the selected fact and page when switching between Brief, Timeline, and Source, including the tablet tab state. Keep doctor confirmation explicit before a fact enters today's note. This is a proposed interaction rule for the project's pilot workflow; it has **not** been clinically validated here. [Prototype workflow](prototype-record-brief/README.md), [pilot brief](pre-visit-record-brief.md)

## Local evaluation that can actually decide

**Stage 1: observe the current visit.** With clinic permission and the appropriate patient process, record 5–10 eligible General Medicine walk-ins with substantial prior records. Count document types/pages, time from check-in to doctor call, which pages the doctor looks for, doctor review plus documentation minutes, and whether the nurse has time to assist. Record phone-file frequency, actual doctor device and resolution, and whether the clinic uses an EHR. This is a proposed discovery sample, **not** a statistical estimate. Use the existing [observation guide](clinic-discovery-and-pilot-observation.md). The Kerala studies warn that carried records can be incomplete, but do not supply these clinic-specific measurements. [Kerala patient-held-record study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/hex.13721), [two-state outpatient study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0207511)

**Stage 2: compare the layouts with safe cases.** Use three matched fictional or properly de-identified record bundles representing: (a) many paper prescriptions with one ambiguous handwritten dose, (b) scattered lab results with an old and a recent value, and (c) a mixed bundle with a missing or late page. Give the same doctor (and, if available, other General Medicine doctors) all three variants in rotated order with a different matched case per variant; repeat enough rotations that a single case or learning effect does not determine the winner. On the clinic's actual desktop and tablet, ask them to identify today's relevant issue, current versus historical medication, one chronology relationship, an uncertain fact, and the original evidence for a key result. Observe without guiding the clicks. Record task time, correct answers, missed important facts, wrong confident assertions, source opens, switching/clicks, and the doctor's preference **after** the tasks. This task-based approach is consistent with the methods in the [problem-oriented retrieval experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33566093/) and [2026 provenance formative study](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2026/1/e95644/), but this specific test is our proposed protocol.

**Stage 3: decide against agreed thresholds.** Before looking at results, ask the doctor what minimum review/documentation time saved is worthwhile and what clinically important omission or wrong fact is unacceptable. Treat a serious miss or wrong medicine/dose as a safety stop for that design until corrected. Report patient and nurse capture time separately from doctor time and the proportion of briefs ready before consultation. If one doctor alone participates, report individual task observations rather than a general “Kerala clinics prefer X” claim. This follows the project's [pilot decision plan](pre-visit-record-brief.md); none of the cited external studies determines local thresholds.

## Evidence limits

The Kerala research concerns selected patients and facilities, much of it public chronic-disease care; it cannot describe the proposed private clinic's devices, staffing, English record quality, or readiness to upload. The UI experiments and guidance come largely from US EHRs, not photographed Kerala paper bundles. The provenance study establishes perceived usability without objective error-detection benefit. The AI summary time study is a preprint and found no significant overall speed gain. **The earlier brief-first recommendation was a usability hypothesis; doctor feedback now favors timeline first, with no measured accuracy or time comparison yet.** The clinic comparison above is necessary before implementation commitment. [Kerala patient-held-record study](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/hex.13721), [problem-oriented retrieval experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33566093/), [2026 provenance study](https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2026/1/e95644), [2025 preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.06.02.25328807v1)
